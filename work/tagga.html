02 · Situation

Group rides fall apart at junctions. Someone misses a turn, the crew stops, phones come out, and a location gets shared as a static pin that is wrong within a minute. The tools that exist (Find My, Life360, Google location sharing) assume a persistent social graph and an account. They were built for families who track each other for months, not for eight people who met at a petrol station and will disband by dinner.

I started Tagga in January 2026 as a rider with this problem. The same shape kept turning up elsewhere: festival crowds, hiking groups, roadtrips. All of them want the same thing: see where everyone is, right now, for a few hours, and then forget it. Tagga is that: one live map for up to thirty people, a five-letter code instead of an account, and a crew chat that ends when the ride does.

It is a one-man project. I own every layer: concept, brand, UX, visual design, frontend, backend and user testing. No team, no external hires. I chose the stack to make that possible: Claude Code with Opus for the heavy lifting, Expo for a single React Native codebase across both platforms, Xcode for the native iOS work. TestFlight and Orbit closed the loop, so a new build reached real beta users within hours and their feedback came back the same day.

03 · Problem

Make a live map a stranger can join in under ten seconds, with gloves on, that stops knowing anything about them when the ride ends.

Constraints — what made it hard
01

No account, no identity layer.

No email, phone number or SSO anywhere in the flow. Whatever identifies a rider has to be created at the roadside and thrown away afterwards.

02

A live map cannot cost you the ride home.

Continuous GPS is the most expensive thing a phone can do. If the battery goes, riders switch the app off at the first fuel stop and it never comes back. So the app runs second-by-second on the bike and sips once you have parked.

03

Readable in one glance at 100 km/h.

Handlebar mount, direct sun, polarised visor. Position, speed and trail have to answer "who is ahead, who is behind, is anyone stopped" without a second look.

04

Three surfaces, one person.

iOS, Android and the share link, shipped from a single codebase by the same person who designed them. Anything that needed a per-platform design pass did not get built.

05

Good roads have no signal.

The routes worth riding are the ones that drop to nothing. Losing signal cannot mean losing the ride, so the app keeps recording through a dead zone and fills the map back in when it returns.

05 · Decisions 04 decisions
D.01

The session is the object. Not the user.

On the table

A user model with friends, groups and history, which is the shape every competitor has. A hybrid where sessions are optional inside a profile.

Why this one

Every real use I observed was bounded: a ride, a hike, a festival day. Modelling the bounded thing directly removed sign-up, contacts, permissions dialogues and the privacy question in one move. A session has a start, a code, participants and an end. That's the whole data model.

What it cost

Nothing survives the ride. Location, messages and notification tokens are dropped with the session, so there is no history to return to, no profile to monetise and no way to re-engage anyone afterwards. A crew that rides every Sunday starts from a new code every Sunday.

D.02

Join by a five-letter code. No accounts.

On the table

Phone-number sign-up with contact sync. Magic-link email. Apple / Google SSO. A QR code on the organiser's screen.

Why this one

A crew forms at a petrol station with gloves on. Anything slower than reading five letters aloud loses the group. Five letters from a 30-character set give 24.3 million codes, enough that two live sessions sharing one is not a practical concern. QR lost because it needs two phones face-to-face; a code works over an intercom.

What it cost

No persistent identity: no ride history, no friends list, no push re-engagement. Retention has to be earned by the next ride, not by a notification.

D.04

Six answers, one tap, with gloves on.

On the table

A keyboard. Voice. The phone-in-your-pocket approach of not communicating mid-ride at all.

Why this one

Everything a rider needs to say at speed is one of six things: wait up, stopping, wrong turn, keep going, I'm OK, in trouble. Fixing that to six taps means the message costs a glove and a second, not a stop. The thread is already populated, because everyone who tapped the link is in it. No numbers to swap, no group to set up.

What it cost

Six options is a closed vocabulary. Anything outside it has to wait for the next stop, and the chat disappears with the session, so nothing said on the ride is there afterwards.

08 · Outcome Honest variant · still in beta
What shipped

A fully functional iOS and Android beta, distributed through TestFlight and Orbit, built solo from concept in January 2026. Crews of up to 30, free, with a waitlist open ahead of the App Store and Google Play release.

What I know

Real beta users have validated it. New builds went out within hours and responses came back the same day, so each loop sharpened the product against behaviour I could watch rather than behaviour I assumed.

What I don't know

Anything at scale. There is no public release yet, so there are no retention, battery or session numbers worth printing. When there are, they go here with their denominators.

The product states plainly that it is not an emergency service.
